title: A Vision Is the Anchor of Life: Reading Be Useful
summary: What a clear vision gives you: whether a decision is good or bad for you depends on whether it moves you closer to, or further from, the life you want.
---

"Vision" can be what people usually call a goal or a dream: the life you want to live, the person you want to become. It can be vague, and become clear as it narrows down and gets refined through practice.

> Think about your hopes, your joys and angers, what you like, what you want to pursue, where you'd like to travel, what kinds of people you'd like to meet, what experiences you'd like to have. All of this will shape your future. — Wing, *Hunter × Hunter*

In the old *Hunter × Hunter* anime, these are the words Wing said to Gon and Killua when they had finished their training at Heavens Arena and were getting ready to leave, and they've stayed with me ever since. In the past I had no concept of a "vision". I just kept searching painfully for a dream, something I wanted to do, a cause to throw myself into, fantasising that once the dream was defined, I'd surely be able to commit to it wholeheartedly and steadily.

Fortunately, and unfortunately too, during the long time when I couldn't find a dream, these words were my guiding light. And only after reading *Be Useful*, and gradually clarifying my dream (or revising it into a more concrete, less cheesy "vision"), did I realise that my vision had been with me all along, deep in my everyday life, and I just hadn't noticed.

Here are a few ideas about vision from the book that I think are important:

> What a clear vision gives you: whether a decision is good or bad for you depends on whether it moves you closer to, or further from, the life you want.

> Good people know exactly what they're trying to achieve, know how to **weigh their choices against their vision**, and are very disciplined about it. They review themselves regularly, and as they change, their vision changes too.

> Stay curious, hunger for information, keep an open mind, and put what you know to use.

If you get the chance to read the book, you'll find the author's discipline and persistence seem to go beyond ordinary people. But you could also read it as: he has a very clear and specific vision, and it gives him a strong drive.

This post mainly picks out a few important passages from the book, adding my own experience and the things I want to remind myself of. Especially the parts about following through, giving it everything and taking action are exactly what I lack the most. It's a reminder to check whether I'm not trying hard enough, to stop making excuses for myself, and to push myself just a little. (And this time, I'm not pushing myself for someone else, but for me!)

---

> Take top athletes as an example. Besides technique, they can visualise the process: they can close their eyes and imagine/see the whole competition.

The visualisation of a vision here probably refers to "motor imagery". I've read about it in articles on climbing training, and I also practise it deliberately before climbing, when I study the route and work out my plan (although I rarely do the most basic thing, filming myself to correct my movement).

I'm quite curious whether climbing, compared with other sports, naturally needs motor imagery more. Because our stamina is limited, we can't keep getting on the wall to test things with our bodies, so we rely more on trying them out in our imagination.

> If you don't **watch** yourself exercise in front of a mirror, you **can't know** whether your form is correct, whether you have enough muscle, or whether the lines are defined enough.

You have to look at yourself properly, objectively and honestly. Only if you're willing to face your mistakes do you have a chance to fix them and get better. You also have to gladly accept your good parts, recognise your strengths and build self-acceptance, and only then do you know how to push yourself in an efficient and effective way, with humility and confidence.

> People who aren't present get to know you through static poses (a magazine, for example). But the people who do come to see you in person, the ones who matter, observe and judge your every move, and the **transitions** between those key moments. It's a perfect metaphor. **Life isn't only about highlights or big moments.**

I like the "transitions" mentioned here. The parts that seem unimportant, ordinary, there just to connect things, may be exactly where you're most likely to relax and show a flaw. Could it be that... the "transitions" are how they judge you, and the key to success or failure?

And to avoid showing flaws in the small details, "showing your true self" is the easiest choice, rather than playing a role, which takes pretending.

> Even if you lose by a hair in the end, it doesn't matter, because you've probably done something quite remarkable along the way.

It's like not being afraid of failed attempts: even if the final result isn't perfect, or is a failure, something definitely didn't fail. For example, you may not win an Olympic gold medal, but your story of struggle inspires many people to take up and care about that sport. And the perseverance you built as an athlete, even after you become a coach or move to another field, lets you hold on longer and handle pressure better than others. It reminds me of *Juhan Shuttai!* (重版出來！), a work about a judo athlete who can't keep chasing the Olympic dream because of an injury and switches to working at a publishing house. Both the original manga and the TV drama are great!

I also like the later point in the book that "in the gym, every time you push yourself to failure is a kind of failure". It seems imperfect every single time, but actually each time you're closer to your goal.

> There's no Plan B. Plan B is successfully completing Plan A. ... For every great dream, Plan B is dangerous, because it exists for failure.

Rather than telling myself "give it everything!" while carrying out a plan, what I want to remind myself of is, before that, when I'm making the plan: "don't choose the easy road."

> I'm passionate about **following through**. ... Important things are often neither simple nor direct, and almost always depend on timing, other people and many variables.
> Use it or **lose it**.

This is a huge reminder for me, because I'm the king of enthusiasm that lasts three minutes (one of the few things I can say with confidence...). It especially reminded me of everything I've studied in the past: French, Spanish, some investment materials and so on. In the end none of it went anywhere or produced any results, like making a foreign friend because of it, passing a proficiency exam, or investing to create some income. If I ever want to pick it up again, I'll definitely have to review a certain amount from scratch, and all that time is a waste that feels like a pity.

> I chose to work as a bricklayer because it was like extra weight training.

The author mentions he had other jobs, to earn money or to save money for the future.

This is worth thinking about. In the best case, "the other job" still helps you reach your vision, but if there's no option that good, be careful not to choose a job that "distracts" you from the vision.

I also love this kind of thing myself: "doing one thing that brings multiple benefits". Bricklaying earns money and is also weight training.

Or take an example from the later part on helping others: the American writer David Sedaris picks up litter while walking, to the point where a garbage truck was named after him and he had afternoon tea with the Queen of England. Picking up litter while walking means exercising while making the environment better. This has nothing to do with a vision, but while meeting your own needs, if it only costs you a bit of effort, almost no loss to yourself, you can help others and beautify the environment... these accumulate easily and unnoticed into a part of your own gains, even achievements. Doesn't that seem like a good deal?

> Present your vision to the world as if it has already come true, and it pushes you to start working hard right now, so that it has to come true.

An idea of first drawing the big pie in the sky, then forcing yourself to make it real!
